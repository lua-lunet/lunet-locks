\begin{abstract}
Unbounded Viewstamped Replication (uVRR) retells David Turner's construction
for keeping a consensus pipeline unbounded through cluster reconfiguration
\cite{turner} in the vocabulary of Viewstamped Replication Revisited
\cite{vrr}: era-indexed ballots, weighted quorums, the leader casting vote,
and unbounded pipelining. The reasoning is Turner's; what this paper adds is
an executable safety ladder --- a Lean 4.33.1 development whose every positive
result is kernel-checked with runnable evidence --- and a working
demonstration. The demonstration matters because the failure is measured,
not hypothetical: Alagappan et al.\ documented consensus-based storage
losing committed data when crashed nodes rejoined as themselves, trusting
state they no longer had
\cite{alagappan2018}. uVRR eliminates that class by design: Crash-Recovery is
converted into Crash-Stop, and a crashed node reincarnates as a new node that
rejoins as a weight-0 standby (a \emph{standby} is a non-voting member: zero
voting weight, part of no quorum, inactive in the Viewstamped Replication
algorithm). The checkpoint extends an inherited Lean development of the
era-indexed agreement argument with a finite-support weighted-majority
theorem, a proposal-era bound from suffix promises, operational acceptor and
normal-log invariants, a whole-log selection induction step, and
fault-injection controls. We distinguish reproducibility from mathematical
correctness and from protocol refinement; counterexamples establish
independence of assumptions from claims of universal necessity. The
outstanding integration obligations are stated explicitly: protocol
refinement, recovery fencing, repeated reconfiguration, client semantics,
and progress. The lunet-locks demonstration implements the diskless core for
advisory leases and is under active hardening; rejoin-latency, forced-flush,
and fault-injection experiments are designed but not yet performed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{V}{iewstamped} Replication (VSR) was introduced by Oki and
Liskov \cite{vsr}: computations run at a primary, which notifies its backups of what
it has done, and when a replica fails the backups reorganize --- a
\emph{view change} --- so that processing continues without user-visible
interruption. Viewstamped Replication Revisited (VRR) presents an updated
version of the protocol \cite{vrr}. It works in an asynchronous network like
the Internet and handles failures in which nodes fail by crashing. It supports
a replicated service running on a number of replica nodes: clients run
general operations to observe and modify the service state, so the approach is
suitable for implementing replicated services such as a lock manager or a
file system. In normal operation the primary orders client operations,
replicates each one with a quorum acknowledgement before committing and
executing it, and answers the client. A view change replaces a failed primary:
replicas exchange their logs in two rounds, and the new primary installs the
log with the greatest last-normal view, breaking ties by length. A crashed
replica may rejoin only after a recovery exchange vouches that its state is
at least as recent as when it failed. A reconfiguration protocol changes the
group membership, and even the number of failures the group can handle, by
moving the group between epochs. Notably, the protocol performs no disk writes
on the normal path: replicated state, not local storage, provides the
persistence.

That diskless design has a sharp edge. Alagappan et al., in a Best Paper at
USENIX FAST 2018 \cite{alagappan2018}, show that consensus-based storage can
break the invariants of the very protocol the node runs when a node recovers
from a crash and rejoins as itself: under the recovery policies the systems
actually shipped, a recovering node truncates its log, losing the data
locally, and when that node then forms a majority together with nodes that
are lagging, committed data is silently lost, violating safety. Their
Section~2.3 states exactly this on the printed page~17; their Figure~2
(page~18) walks the failure through the protocol step by step; and their
Table~4(a) (page~25) measures it, with the shipped recovery policies
recovering correctly in only 46 of 2401 targeted-corruption cases across the
systems they tested. Their protocol-aware recovery repairs the class by
teaching the storage layer the protocol's own rules, at the cost of fsyncs on
the recovery boundary. Michael
et al.\ had formalized the same boundary for diskless crash recovery
\cite{diskless,recovery}; the Appendix B.1 divergence pattern of their
extended report is the same class.

This paper takes the position that the amnesia class is better eliminated by
design than repaired by a smarter recovery path. The motivating uVRR proposal
asks whether a strongly consistent replicated service can replace failed
members while continuing to process requests, without forcing its log to disk
on the normal agreement path \cite{uvrr}. Unbounded Viewstamped Replication
answers by converting Crash-Recovery into Crash-Stop: a low-latency cluster
reconfiguration evicts the crashed identity through a forced sequence of
unit weight changes, and the node \emph{reincarnates} on restart as a new
node that rejoins as a weight-0 standby (a standby has zero voting weight, so
it cannot form part of any quorum nor actively participate in the VSR
algorithm) and earns its votes back under the leader's control. Same-identity
recovery after volatile-state loss is thereby unrepresentable: the crashed
identity is gone, so no recovered-but-amnesiac process is ever trusted again.
The network round trips of reincarnation replace the fsync, because ``the
network is faster than the disk'' \cite{netdisk}. The original contribution at
stake is diskless strong consistency throughout VRR view changes, with
replicated state, not local storage, providing the persistence.

A statement of attribution comes early. This paper represents David Turner's
Unbounded Paxos leader casting-vote techniques \cite{turner}, retold here as
Unbounded Viewstamped Replication: the construction --- era-indexed ballots,
weighted quorums, the leader casting vote, unbounded pipelining through
reconfiguration --- is his, and the reasoning is his, ported from his
dynamically reconfigurable consensus clusters to the VRR setting. Crediting a
mathematical technique and applying it to a distinct algorithm is valid: Paxos
is not VRR. Turner does
not use the abbreviation ``UPaxos''; this paper does not either. What is new
here is the executable safety ladder: every inherited and extended result is
replayed as a kernel-checked Lean 4.33.1 development with executable evidence
documents, negative controls, and model checks. It is also new here as a
working demonstration: lunet-locks, an advisory-lease service running the
diskless core, described in Section~\ref{sec:demo}. The experiments planned
for that demonstration --- node-kill rejoin latency, the latency delta of a
forced synchronous disk flush at the recovery boundary, Maelstrom
brute-force fault injection at three and then five nodes, and repetition on
cloud deployments --- are presented in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} as designs,
with placeholders in place of measurements and warnings at the section's top
and bottom.

A precise safety answer requires more than an intersection calculation. The
membership used by a vote must be agreed; a replacement primary must preserve
committed operations; a crashed replica must not cast votes using forgotten
state; and a reply must correspond to an operation with the promised client
semantics. Progress and elapsed time impose additional assumptions. The
central unresolved claim is stated explicitly: \emph{every execution of the
intended uVRR message protocol, under a specified crash-recovery model,
implements a linearizable replicated state machine across arbitrarily many
reconfigurations}. The results below discharge parts of this claim --- as
kernel-checked component theorems, stated obligations, and a demonstration ---
as of this checkpoint. They do not discharge it by renaming a ballot a VRR
view.

\section{Setting and trust boundary}
Let $A$, $B$, and $V$ be node, ballot, and value types. A node set is a
predicate $A\to\mathrm{Prop}$; a quorum family is a predicate on such sets.
Define
\[
 Q\inter R\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\forall q\in Q,\ r\in R,\quad q\cap r\ne\varnothing.
\]
This relation is symmetric. It is a property of two families, not a requirement
that every pair of members within either family intersect. Empty families
make some such statements vacuous. Consequently nonemptiness conditions in
the agreement theorem must remain visible.

The history model records messages that have ever been emitted. Its predicates
include free promises $p(a,b)$, promises carrying a last vote $p(a,b;c)$,
proposals, acceptances, and choices. A function $v(b)$ gives the unique value
associated with each ballot. The representation presupposes ballot ownership
or an equivalent mechanism preventing two values at one ballot; it is not a
proof that a wire protocol enforces that mechanism.

The ballot order is well founded and strictly total. The era development uses
$B=\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ ordered lexicographically, so that unboundedly
many rounds are available in each era. This is a mathematical domain; a
fixed-width implementation needs a separate overflow argument.

The executable library imports Lean core and other modules in the same
development. It has no Mathlib dependency. A proof may use standard Lean
axioms \code{propext}, \code{Quot.sound}, and \code{Classical.choice}; these
are distinguished from additional protocol hypotheses appearing in theorem
types. Printing axioms alone does not expose every protocol assumption: a
conditional theorem can have an empty axiom footprint and strong hypotheses.
The central generic single-instance theorem has no axiom dependencies. Other
results use subsets of the three standard axioms.

\section{The single-instance agreement argument}\label{sec:synod}
Turner's construction, following the single-instance argument he recasts from
Lamport's Synod, rests on six history conditions. Fig.~\ref{fig:synod} shows
the invariants preserved by the single-instance agreement argument, redrawn
with attribution from Turner's construction and restated here in our
notation; they are encoded as \code{Synod.Inv}.

 \Rightarrow \QI(b_1)\inter\QII(b_2)$\\[2pt]

The maximal reported ballot in S4 is supplied existentially. An
implementation must obtain it from a finite collection and enforce the value
rule. The history theorem does not construct a leader's message handler.

\begin{theorem}[Synod agreement]
Assume the six conditions, a well-founded strict total ballot order, and
nonempty phase-II quorums. If $b$ and $c$ are chosen, then $v(b)=v(c)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a chosen $c$ and a proposed $b>c$, intersect the decision quorum at $c$
with the promise quorum at $b$. A common acceptor cannot have supplied a free
promise at $b$, since it accepted $c$. Its reported vote is at least $c$ by
condition 3. The greatest reported ballot $m$ therefore satisfies
$c\le m<b$. If $m=c$, condition 4 gives the conclusion. Otherwise apply
well-founded induction to the proposal at $m$, which exists by conditions 3
and 5. Finally, a chosen ballot has a proposal by conditions 5 and 6 and
decision-quorum nonemptiness. Trichotomy reduces agreement to the preceding
argument or equality of ballots.
\end{proof}
The checked declaration is \code{Synod.theorem8}; its intermediate induction
lemma is \code{Synod.lemma7}. This proof follows Turner's Appendix B.

Turner weakens the classical requirement that all majorities intersect: the
phase-I and phase-II quorum families of one ballot need intersect only for
the relevant ballot pairs (his section III-B). That weakening is what makes
general reconfiguration possible; the inherited development encodes it as
S1 rather than as a global intersection assumption.

\section{Era-indexed agreement and unbounded pipelining}\label{sec:eras}
VRR runs a sequence of agreement instances, one per log position; Turner's
construction indexes their quorum families by \emph{eras}, which play the
role of VRR's epochs. Fig.~\ref{fig:eras} shows the invariants preserved by
the era-indexed agreement argument, redrawn with attribution from Turner's
construction and restated here in our notation.

\subsection{The configuration condition}
Let $e(b)$ be a ballot's era and $e(i)$ the era of instance $i$. Phase I uses
$\QI_{e(b)}$ and phase II uses $\QII_{e(i)}$. The configuration condition is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p1}
 \forall e,\qquad \QII_e\inter\QI_e
 \quad\land\quad \QI_e\inter\QII_{e+1}.
\end{equation}
The reduction also requires proposal and choice era bounds:
\begin{align}
 \proposed_i(b)&\Longrightarrow e(b)\le e(i),\label{eq:erale}\\
 \chosen_i(b)&\Longrightarrow e(i)\le e(b)+1.\label{eq:eraup}
\end{align}
Ballot order must respect era order. These conditions are separate from
Equation~\eqref{eq:p1}; the latter alone is not a complete protocol contract.

\begin{lemma}[Relevant intersection]
If $b_2<b_1$, $b_1$ is proposed at $i$, and $b_2$ is chosen at $i$, then
$\QI_{e(b_1)}\inter\QII_{e(i)}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The bounds give $e(b_1)\le e(i)\le e(b_2)+1\le e(b_1)+1$.
Thus $e(i)$ is either $e(b_1)$ or $e(b_1)+1$. The first case uses the
intra-era half of Equation~\eqref{eq:p1}, by symmetry; the second uses its
cross-era half.
\end{proof}
Instantiating the single-instance theorem for every instance yields
\code{Paxos.theorem10} (the inherited namespace name is the original
development's). Its type also contains the promise, value-selection,
acceptance, and choice conditions. Agreement holds for every history
satisfying them, across any number of eras. This quantification does not
establish that an operational sequence of membership changes preserves them.

\subsection{Deriving the proposal-era bound}
The inherited \code{EraLe} hypothesis, Equation~\eqref{eq:erale}, bypassed
part of Turner's Figure 2. The extension \code{MultiPromise.History} restores a
known-instance frontier $i_{\max}$ and suffix promises $p_{\ge j}(a,b)$.
A suffix certificate expands to a free promise at every $i\ge j$.

\begin{theorem}[Suffix-promise reduction]
Suppose instance eras are nondecreasing. A promise at $j$, including a suffix
promise, satisfies $e(b)\le e(\min(j,i_{\max}))$. Each proposal at $i$ has a
nonempty phase-I quorum whose members supply a free promise at $i$, a last-vote
report at $i$, or a suffix promise starting at some $j\le i$. Then
$\proposed_i(b)\Rightarrow e(b)\le e(i)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose a member of the proposal quorum. For a point promise,
$\min(i,i_{\max})\le i$. For a suffix promise,
$\min(j,i_{\max})\le j\le i$. In either case monotonicity of instance eras
transports the promise's era bound to $e(i)$.
\end{proof}
\code{MultiPromise.History.expanded\_inv} additionally transports the full
promise history to the inherited invariant structure. The resulting
\code{agreement} theorem retains the known-frontier bound for choices.
The expansion is an invariant reduction; the append-only evolution of
configuration knowledge and the creation of suffix certificates still need an
operational proof. A single certificate can denote every slot $j+d$ for
$d\in\mathbb N$, as checked by \code{suffix\_unbounded}. This proves
representational unboundedness, not completion of those slots.

\subsection{Dynamic configuration changes and the casting vote}
In Turner's construction the configuration sequence and the era sequence are
themselves chosen by agreement and held inside the replicated state machine,
so no external configuration oracle exists; the sequences may only be
appended to, and a node may promise for instances whose era is not yet
decided, because the promise is bounded by $e(\min(i,i_{\max}))$ rather than
by a known $e(i)$. In normal running the leader holds promises from a
phase-I quorum for one large ballot $b$ whose era matches the frontier
$e(i_{\max})$, and may then propose for every instance without limit. A
reconfiguration appends the replacement configuration as the next era and
lets the frontier advance past $e(b)$: proposals on $b$ remain decidable
because $e(i)\le e(b)+1$, so no pipeline bound is needed. The leader
restores normal running by completing phase I at a ballot $b'$ of the new
era, sending \code{prepare($b'$)} to the new quorum minus itself while the
members of the old decision quorum keep accepting on $b$.

The enabling structure is the \emph{casting vote}: a leader $\ell$ such that
the old decision quorum $q$ and the new promise quorum $q'$ intersect in
$\ell$ alone. The inherited casting-vote lemma (rung 6) assumes $q$, $q'$,
and a leader $\ell$ with $q\cap q'=\{\ell\}$. Sending new promises only to
$q'\setminus\{\ell\}$ leaves the old acceptance guard unaffected at the other
members of $q$. Once $\ell$ promises, the new quorum is complete --- the
leader's self-message has no network delay. This is a schedule-dependent
noninterference argument. Availability of those quorums, continued processing
by the leader, successful message delivery, and the ability to repeat the
procedure require additional proofs. The lemma includes no clock or duration.
Rung 23 instantiates the same structure on the reincarnation eras of
Section~\ref{sec:reincarnation}: in the two-node leader-overlap case --- the
load-bearing one --- the eras' quorum families meet in the leader alone and
every era-boundary quorum pair is decisive at the leader, while the degenerate
three-node case's majorities coincide, so it needs no pivot and exercises no
casting vote.

Fig.~\ref{fig:unbounded} shows the message flow of a fully concurrent
reconfiguration of the Unbounded Viewstamped Replication cluster, redrawn
with attribution from Turner's Figure~3: the leader $\ell$ continues
proposing on ballot $b$ across the era boundary while the new era's phase-I
exchange is outstanding, client requests keep being serviced on the old
ballot, and the pipeline never stops. Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic} shows the same
reconfiguration in the bounded-pipeline regime that Turner contrasts with his
construction --- his paper's term for it was Dynamic Paxos with $\alpha = 2$
(his Figure~4, redrawn with attribution): the pause between instances
$i{+}5$ and $i{+}6$ is the pipeline bound waiting on the new era's phase-I
exchange. Fig.~\ref{fig:stoppable} shows the same reconfiguration in the
stop-and-reconfigure regime --- his paper's term for it was Stoppable Paxos
(his Figure~5, redrawn with attribution): the reconfiguration command at
$i{+}3$ is proposed before the two preceding instances, and client request
processing suspends once the free instances are used up.

\section{Weighted configurations}\label{sec:weights}
For a finite support list $L$ and natural weights $w$, let
$W=\sum_{a\in L}w(a)$ and $W_q=\sum_{a\in L\cap q}w(a)$. Define
$M_L(w)=\{q:2W_q>W\}$. A duplicate-free list gives the usual finite-set
interpretation; repeated entries instead contribute repeated weight. No bound
on the cardinality of $L$ is fixed in the theorem.

\begin{lemma}[Disjoint selection bound]\label{lem:disjoint}
For disjoint $q,r$ and natural weights $x,y$,
\[
 2X_q+2Y_r\le X+Y+\sum_{a\in L}|x(a)-y(a)|.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At each node, at most one of the two selections contributes. The required
pointwise inequality follows from $2x\le x+y+|x-y|$ or its symmetric form;
if neither selects the node it is immediate. Sum by induction over $L$.
The Lean encoding uses $(x-y)+(y-x)$ with natural truncated subtraction.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Scaled weighted-majority overlap]\label{thm:weights}
For positive integers $k,l$, if
\[
 D=\sum_{a\in L}|k w(a)-l w'(a)|\le1,
\]
then $M_L(w)\inter M_L(w')$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Positive rescaling preserves strict majority. If two majorities were
disjoint, their scaled sums would exceed their respective scaled totals by
at least one each, since all sums are integers. Their combined excess is at
least two, contradicting Lemma~\ref{lem:disjoint} and $D\le1$.
\end{proof}
This is the weighted-intersection lemma of Turner's Appendix A, proved here
by a pointwise discrepancy bound. \code{WeightedGeneral.scaled\_overlap} is
the checked declaration. The unit-change result, equal-rescaling result, and
self-intersection follow as corollaries. Zero total weight yields an empty
majority family, so self-intersection by itself does not assert availability.
The intersection property is the trivial part of the argument: majorities
over integer weights that differ by at most one unit per node must overlap, by
the pigeonhole principle, and the arithmetic above is all it takes. The real
burden is leader overlap across eras --- the same value can reach a new era
accepted under a different ballot of a different leader, the recovery nuance
covered in the author's Paxos posts \cite{trexposts} --- and it is that
overlap, not the integer arithmetic, that the surviving proofs must carry.

The uniform bound is sharp. With two nodes, weights $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ have
distance two and admit disjoint singleton majorities. This is checked by
\code{distance\_two\_counterexample}. Some distance-two changes nevertheless
preserve overlap; for example $(2,2,2,0)$ to $(4,2,2,0)$ does. Thus sharpness
means that a larger universal allowance would admit counterexamples, not that
all changes beyond the allowance are unsafe.

The inherited four-node weighted schedule remains an independent executable
example. Its seven rows obey the required overlaps. It is useful for a
concrete deployment shape; it is no longer the only evidence for the general
weighted construction. Turner's worked example motivates the same schedule:
a naive three-era node swap in a four-node cluster needs all four
infrastructure domains independent and leaves the middle era without a
casting vote, while the seven-era weight sequence achieves the same swap
letting the old and new nodes share infrastructure, with casting votes held
throughout. His account of one-at-a-time membership changes under unit
weights is the same distance-one rule checked here: under weights in
$\{0,1\}$, consecutive majorities overlap when a single node's weight
changes by one, and the weight-$1\ldots n$ family shows why no larger change
is safe. Weightless configurations always satisfy the configuration
condition against anything, though an instance decided by an empty family can
never be chosen; consecutive instance eras may also skip, which forces a new
phase I before any new phase II and recovers the bounded regime of
Fig.~\ref{fig:stoppable}.

\section{Operational promises and fault controls}\label{sec:operational}
\subsection{A checked acceptor transition system}
An acceptor state is $(f,H,R)$: a promise watermark $f\in\mathbb N$, a
newest-first vote history $H$, and a reply history $R$. Initially these are
$(0,[],[])$. Acceptance requires $f\le b$; a promise requires $f<b$.
The transitions are:
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{accept}_b(f,H,R)&=(b,b::H,R),\\
 \operatorname{promise}_b(f,H,R)&=(b,H,(b,\operatorname{head?}H)::R).
\end{align*}
An empty head produces a free reply; otherwise the reply carries the latest
accepted ballot. The histories are ghost evidence for the proof. This module
does not prescribe retaining an unbounded physical list at a replica.

\begin{theorem}[Historical promise safety]
In every state reachable by a finite execution, a free reply at $b$ excludes
votes below $b$. A reply carrying $c$ at $b$ establishes $c<b$, an acceptance
of $c$, and absence of acceptances strictly between $c$ and $b$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Strengthen the induction invariant: all votes are at most the watermark;
the head vote dominates its tail; every emitted reply's ballot is at most the
watermark; and each reply satisfies the stated historical conditions.
An acceptance preserves every old reply because its ballot is at least the
old watermark. A promise reads the maximal vote and raises the watermark
above it. Neither transition removes prior evidence. Induction on reachable
states proves the claim.
\end{proof}
The declarations are \code{Acceptor.reachable\_inv}, \code{free\_forbids},
and \code{report\_last}. They discharge S2 and S3 for a single-era acceptor.
They do not establish the proposal value rule, an entire log's view-change
selection, or restoration after volatile-state loss. Extending the component
to era-tagged views also remains an explicit integration task.

\subsection{What the negative controls establish}
The controls separate two questions: whether the proof notices an altered
definition, and whether removing an assumption admits an inconsistent history.
The first is measured by compiler rejection; the second by constructing and
checking a counterexample with the remaining conditions intact.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Cross-era overlap.} The inherited two-node history satisfies
      intra-era overlap and the other encoded era-history conditions, but
      chooses both Boolean values at one slot when the cross overlap is absent.
\item \emph{Value selection.} One acceptor reports ballot 0 at ballot 1,
      yet the two proposals use different values. All listed single-instance
      conditions except S4 hold and both ballots are chosen.
\item \emph{Promise fencing.} One acceptor supplies free promises at two
      rounds despite its earlier vote. All listed conditions except S2 hold,
      and agreement fails.
\item \emph{Nonempty decisions.} Empty phase-II quorums permit choices with
      no proposal or acceptance. The six history invariants hold vacuously
      where appropriate, and the two selected values differ.
\item \emph{Operational guard.} After a free promise at round 2, accepting
      round 1 without checking the watermark violates the reachable invariant.
\end{itemize}
These examples establish independence within the specified contracts.
They do not prove a universal necessity theorem over all consensus algorithms,
nor do they enumerate every combination of omitted assumptions.

Separately, the mutation harness replaces the checker's universal quantifier
over quorums by an existential one, and replaces a safe concrete quorum family
by an unsafe family. Lean rejects each altered source while accepting the
unchanged control. An injected \code{sorry} demonstrates a different failure
mode: compilation succeeds but \code{\#print axioms} exposes \code{sorryAx}.
The harness bounds child memory, work and wall time and modifies only temporary
copies. Infrastructure failures are not counted as killed mutants.

\section{The boundary between Unbounded Viewstamped Replication and VRR-2012}
\label{sec:boundary}
VRR-2012 is a replication protocol, with client handling and recovery in
addition to the agreement mechanism \cite{vrr}. Its normal path requires
ordered log preparation and a quorum of acknowledgements before execution.
View change selects the log with the greatest last-normal view, breaking ties
by length. That whole-log selection rule needs a proof relating logs from
different views; a per-slot maximal-vote rule is not automatically the same
operation.

The distinction becomes essential after crashes. A recovering replica is
excluded from normal processing and view changes. Its recovery exchange
requires responses from distinct peers, a fresh nonce, and state from the
primary of the greatest reported view. The preceding round of
StartViewChange messages provides replicated knowledge of view advancement.
The paper's Section 8.2 gives a failure scenario when that round is removed.
Replacing volatile state by an acceptor watermark that never disappears
would bypass precisely this obligation.

The 2012 reconfiguration protocol stops taking new client requests in the old
epoch, transfers the committed state, and activates the replacement group.
Section 7.5 explicitly discusses the resulting delay and prewarming as a way
to reduce it. The uVRR objective instead calls for progress during the
transition. Equation~\eqref{eq:p1} and casting-vote scheduling address part
of that difference, while the interaction with recovery remains to be proved.

\subsection{Checked whole-log selection}
The executable \code{ViewSelection.select} takes a seed report and a list of
additional reports, enforcing nonemptiness without a fallback value. It selects
by last-normal view and then log length. Each report's committed frontier is
bounded by its length in the report type. The checked results establish that
the output belongs to the input collection and has maximal rank. For reports
of length at most $M$, \code{scalar\_rank\_equivalent} proves that the
model's scalar rank $v(M+1)+n$ has exactly the same ordering. A counterexample
shows that omitting the length bound can misrank reports.

The prefix-preservation result is the induction step for a single era. Let a
prefix $P$ have committed in view $v$. Intersect its commit quorum with the
reporting quorum and obtain a report from a common sender. The explicit
voter-history premise says that this report has retained view at least $v$
and, at equality, contains $P$. The induction hypothesis supplies preservation
for reports from strictly later normal views. If the selected report is later
than $v$, that hypothesis applies. Otherwise the witness and selected report
are both from $v$. Same-view log comparability and maximal selected length
then imply that the witness log, and hence $P$, is a prefix of the selected
log. \code{ViewSelection.quorum\_preserves} checks this argument.

The selector itself only compares ranks. The surviving-voter and log-origin
premises still require proofs from message and recovery transitions. A
two-report fault control admits divergent uncommitted suffixes in different
views: an older two-entry log loses to a newer one-entry log. A length-only
selector chooses the older log and loses the newer committed entry. These
results close the selection subproblem while preserving the distinction
between a checked induction step and a complete reachable-state proof.

\subsection{Normal-log message provenance}
\texttt{NormalLog} first checks the fixed-view message argument. The primary
starts from an arbitrary base and appends. StartView installs that base once;
normal replicas accept authentic prepares at their next slot and emit reports
copying their logs. Persistent messages permit delayed, reordered, and duplicate
delivery attempts. The invariant makes replica logs, sent prepare prefixes,
and reports prefixes of the primary history. At receipt, the replica and
pre-send logs are equal-length prefixes of that history, hence equal; appending
the sent entry preserves the invariant. The pre-send log is ghost evidence,
not a content check in the handler. \texttt{reports\_comparable} follows from
prefix comparability, and \texttt{replica\_history} proves retention over finite
continuations. Bypassing next-slot checking admits an authentic second entry
at slot one. This component supplies the local argument integrated below;
its arbitrary initial base alone does not justify cross-view safety.

\subsection{Shared multi-view preservation}
The next integration is now checked. \texttt{ViewFence} models a local replica
installing higher normal views, appending in normal mode, raising its floor
before reporting, and retaining immutable vote and reply histories. Its
\texttt{report\_covers\_vote} theorem bounds every historical vote below a
report's target by the reported last-normal view; at equality, the voted log
is a prefix of the report. This remains true of votes later in the trace.
The selector therefore consumes a derived voter-history property. Strong
induction discharges the strictly-later-view premise over activated views;
a concrete two-view execution demonstrates that the interface is inhabited.

\texttt{LogProvenance} joins the local histories to a shared message model.
Each view has one immutable installation base and an append-only primary
history. Activation requires a quorum of authentic reports and uses the
executable latest-normal-view/length selector. An issued prepare carries a
view, next slot, and entry; its pre-send log is ghost evidence. Installation
reads an activated base. Receipt checks normal mode, view equality, and the
next slot. Fencing and report emission update the local replica state.
There is no guard testing global committed-prefix agreement.

The invariant establishes that each replica log, historical vote, and report
extends its retained view's base and is a prefix of that view's primary
history. Each activated base retains its selection certificate because report
histories persist. These facts discharge both same-view comparability and
base-extension provenance in the view induction.
\texttt{later\_base\_preserves} proves preservation by every later activated
base. \texttt{committed\_comparable} proves that any two quorum-voted prefixes
in a reachable state are comparable; \texttt{committed\_equal} specializes
this to equal-length prefix equality. The model bounds neither log length,
view numbers, nor activation count, and fixes one self-overlapping quorum
family. A concrete trace commits a nonempty prefix in two successive views.
A delayed old-view prepare has the expected next slot after installation;
bypassing view equality permits an unsupported new-view vote. The constructive
control and source mutation both check this boundary.

This is a complete safety induction for the stated crash-free shared model,
not yet the requested uVRR theorem. Unique source activation is enforced by an
unused-view guard, and source histories may continue issuing prepares after
replicas have fenced that view. Their eventual acceptance is constrained by
replica guards. A refinement must justify these abstractions from unique
primary activation and the concrete wire protocol. Crashes, first-round
view-change knowledge, arbitrary membership changes, client responses, and
progress remain outside this model. Ghost histories do not imply durable
storage at replicas, but they do not prove recovery after volatile-state loss.

\subsection{Recovery fencing: a checked boundary}
\texttt{RecoveryFence} proves a narrower persistence result. Suppose a fixed
support quorum is simultaneously online with bounds at least $b$, and
historical responses carry causal recipient-episode identifiers. Crashing
erases a local bound and advances the environment's fresh episode identifier;
recovering nodes cannot answer. Recovery uses distinct current-episode
responders and chooses a maximal bound. Induction proves that every online
member of the support retains a bound at least $b$ through arbitrary finite
crash/recovery continuations. The quorum checkpoint is a premise, not a
consequence of collecting asynchronous view-change messages. The model does
not restore logs or establish progress. A stale-episode witness and a source
mutation check the freshness boundary.

Classic diskless crash-recovery designs are exposed to a known failure
pattern: Michael et al.\ describe delayed view-change and recovery
divergence in Appendix B.1 and Figure 1 of their extended report
\cite{diskless,recovery}; a replica forgets that it sent a view-change
message and recovers into a lower view, and the delayed message later lands
under a new leader that overwrites committed decisions. That is literature
about the classic
crash-recover class, not an open bug here: uVRR eliminates the class by
construction. A crashed node whose durable superblocks record an unflushed
session reopens under a new incarnation (Crash-Stop-Self-Evict), the leader
evicts the old identity, and the new identity rejoins as a weight-0 standby
that may receive streams but whose messages are ignored. Same-identity
recovery after volatile-state loss is therefore unrepresentable, and the
stale-episode rule above is precisely the ingress rule the reincarnated
standby obeys. Their crash-vector construction provides the collection
primitive the reincarnation leader needs.

\subsection{Crash-vector collection}
Following Algorithm 1 and Definition 6 of the extended report
\cite{recovery}, let a reply $r$ contain sender $s_r$, vector
$v_r:A\to\mathbb N$, and a payload. The collector stores a known vector $k$
and replies $R$. Upon receiving $r$, it computes
\[
\begin{aligned}
 k'(a)&=\max(k(a),v_r(a)),\\
 R'&=\{q\in r::R\mid k'(s_q)\le v_q(s_q)\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The implementation uses a list; quorum membership is the set of sender
identities. Thus duplicates do not create additional identities. Induction
proves every retained vector is pointwise bounded by $k$. The filter gives
the converse inequality at each retained reply's own sender. Consequently,
\[
 \forall r,q\in R,\qquad v_r(s_q)\le v_q(s_q),
\]
the published crash-consistency condition. A known-stale reply remains
ineligible as the frontier grows. A concrete three-node example shows that
an unfiltered two-sender quorum violates this condition; filtering removes
the stale reply and a current replacement restores collector quorum size.
Removing the filter in a temporary source copy causes Lean rejection.

This is a collector theorem, not a recovery theorem. The published protocol
requires recovery itself to acquire and propagate its new incarnation through
a crash-consistent quorum; in uVRR that acquisition is the reincarnation path
the four-superblock identity contract drives. Its quorum-knowledge persistence argument also
covers reconstruction, message freshness and resending after pruning. Those
obligations are not discharged by attaching vectors to ordinary VRR replies.
The report's termination condition requires an appropriate quorum stable over
the acquisition interval; arbitrary churn with a bound on simultaneous
recoveries alone is not a termination theorem. Mechanizing that temporal
argument and composing it with log safety remain open.

\subsection{Acquisition ordering and operational certificates}
\texttt{AcquisitionOrder} checks the secondary induction of Theorem 3 in the
extended report \cite{recovery}. With asynchronous per-participant witness
times and a generic stable property, an online participant either retains its
witness knowledge or inherits it from an earlier response quorum. Quorum
intersection and forward ordering of those responses relative to the witness
events discharge a strong induction over time, using core Lean's
\code{Nat.strongRecOn}. A separate lemma derives that forward ordering from
crash-vector consistency, conditional on recovery participants retaining
incarnation knowledge. A countermodel satisfies every other origin condition
except forward ordering and loses the property; a source mutation that
removes the ordering premise is rejected.

\texttt{RecoveryAcquire} then makes the acquisition operational, following
Algorithm 1. Crash, start, emit, answer, collect and finish transitions act on
an immutable authenticated message history. A crash erases all protocol
knowledge and begins a fresh logical incarnation; request identity is an
explicit echoed (incarnation, sequence) pair in addition to the response
vector check. Induction over reachable states proves that every finished
certificate is a crash-consistent quorum for exactly its request and
incarnation, and that every historical reply originated at an operational
sender. A three-node trace completes recovery at incarnation 1. An authentic
reply from the same incarnation but an older request violates freshness when
replayed into a new acquisition, which separates sequence freshness from
incarnation freshness. Mutations that answer from a recovering sender or
accept a stale request are rejected.

These are acquisition mechanics with one active acquisition per node.
Quorum-knowledge persistence across the acquisition interval, value
reconstruction, the read quorum, mapping the echoed (incarnation, sequence)
identity to the superblock incarnation contract, log integration and liveness
remain open.

\subsection{Unbounded Viewstamped Replication reincarnation}\label{sec:reincarnation}
uVRR does not implement crash-recovery. It enforces Crash-Stop-Self-Evict, called
\emph{reincarnation}: a node whose volatile state was lost reopens as a different
node. Durable state is four TigerBeetle-style superblocks. A restart that reads all
four as \code{flushed} marks them \code{unflushed} and continues normally; a restart
that reads any \code{unflushed} block is dirty, bumps its incarnation, and rewrites
all four blocks as the new identity marked \code{flushed}. Any read that observes a
higher identity adopts it. Once the eviction is initiated it must continue. A
\emph{reincarnation} message carries the identity pair (old, new) to the leader; the
durable incarnation supersedes the fence machinery's ghost generation as the
freshness carrier where the two meet, while the fence and acquisition theorems above
remain in force with the carrier re-instantiated.

The leader stays live for client requests and drives a forced weight sequence from
the voting-weights rules \cite{votingweights}: the exiting node's weight goes
$1\to0$ (a Leave), the new identity joins at weight $0$ (a Join), and the new
identity goes $0\to1$. Each step
is a unit-weight change, so the strict majorities of consecutive configurations
overlap by Theorem~\ref{thm:weights}'s corollaries and every intermediate era is
quorum-safe on its own; a leader dying mid-sequence leaves only safe eras that a
new leader can continue. Table~\ref{tab:reincarnationweights} instantiates the
sequence at unit weight for the three-voter cluster $A,B,C$ with $B$ the leader
and $C$ the crashed identity that returns as $D$: the start configuration is
$(1,1,1,\textendash)$; era~1 drives the leader's batch
\code{DECREMENT(C)}~+~\code{JOIN(D)}, reaching $(1,1,0,0)$; era~2 drives
\code{INCREMENT(D)}~+~\code{LEAVE(C)}, reaching $(1,1,\textendash,1)$. In the
table a \textendash{} is not a weight: it marks an identity that is not a member
of the configuration in that era, whereas $0$ is the weight of a standby member
that the cluster still holds in its order --- which the reincarnated node is
between the two eras while it is streamed preemptively. The old identity $C$ is
evicted by Crash-Stop-Self-Evict, never a recovered amnesiac. If the sequence
runs in the doubled scale of a weight-doubling
plan, the return halve is integral only when all weights are even, so the corner
sequence inserts one extra unit increment on the new identity before the halve. The
leader streams the first reconfiguration, then client traffic, and preemptively
streams to the reincarnated node as a weight-0 standby; messages to it are fine and
messages from it are discarded by the standard membership check, which is the same
rule the crash-vector collector's stale-reply filter realizes. If the leader crashes,
the cluster must reach a stable leader before the reincarnated node forces its old
identity's eviction.

Fig.~\ref{fig:reincarnation} shows this forced sequence as a space-time chart
drawn in the grammar of Turner's message diagrams \cite{turner}: four

The economics defer disk flushes: nano-state fits in memory, so disk flushes can
be deferred, and the network round trips of reincarnation are cheaper than fsyncs
because ``the network is faster than the disk'' \cite{netdisk}; the four-superblock
mark is one fsync amortized over the whole epoch.
Same-identity recovery after volatile-state loss is thereby unrepresentable rather
than repaired: classic VRR-2012 diskless quorum recovery is not performed, so the
class that design exposes --- Michael et al.'s Appendix B.1
divergence pattern \cite{recovery} and the truncate data-loss finding of
Alagappan et al.'s Section~2.3 \cite{alagappan2018} --- is avoided by
construction, and a zero-weight
standby may speculatively recover its log non-votingly as future work. The
formalization of the superblock contract and the forced sequence against the fence
and acquisition machinery is a concrete proof obligation, recorded with the
reincarnation rung's stated obligations.

\section{The demonstration: lunet-locks}\label{sec:demo}
The demonstration is \code{lunet-locks}, a runnable, fixed-membership
advisory-lock service. It orders lock requests with \code{vrr-core}, the
sans-IO Viewstamped Replication Revisited core that accompanies this
formalization: one total function maps a tick, an input message, and a state
to a new state and the messages to send, and the host owns time, transport,
storage, packetization, threading, and naming. Clients speak
newline-delimited JSON over TCP; replicas exchange the core's binary
datagrams over raw UDP; the service process is LuaJIT and libuv (the Lunet
runtime, version-pinned and hash-verified), and a small Rust adapter drives
the core and owns the lock state machine.

\subsection{Components}
The adapter instantiates the concrete core replica over a segmented log with
weighted-majority quorums, running volatile stability: protocol state lives
in quorum memory, an empty journal is provisioned at boot, and the only bytes
the service ever fsyncs locally are the recovery nonce file. The adapter
retains the tick clock (a monotonic value clamped against wall-clock
regression), exactly-once reply correlation keyed by the client's message
identifier, and guard checks that re-validate every replicated operation
entry --- in Prepare, DoViewChange, StartView, RecoveryResponse, and NewState
messages --- against the lock service before the entry reaches the core. The
Teal/Lunet wrapper owns sockets, TCP framing, peer source validation, and
forwarding; a UDP peer envelope carrying a membership fingerprint guards the
fixed membership at the transport boundary, so differently configured
clusters cannot merge. An append-only lock-event journal, a REST/WebSocket
feed, and a console are observability only: the journal never participates in
replication or recovery, and a journal error disables journaling without
affecting the service path.

\subsection{The rejoin path implemented}
Every boot enters fenced recovery stamped with a fresh durable nonce. The
nonce file is updated atomically --- write, fsync, rename, parent-directory
sync --- and the new value is used as the recovery input's tick, so
per-node monotonicity holds across the two clock sources. Recovery needs a
live quorum: a restarted node rejoins from quorum memory, installing the
latest fenced view's history and re-applying its committed suffix. A rolling
single-node restart therefore recovers from the surviving members, while a
simultaneous full-cluster loss forfeits whatever the quorum held, and an
operator re-bootstrapping a lost cluster must do so only after all
outstanding leases can no longer be valid.

Membership is fixed for one process lifetime, so the reincarnation eviction
of Section~\ref{sec:reincarnation} --- the forced weight sequence that
replaces the crashed identity with a new one --- is not exercised by this
service. What the demonstration implements is the classic recovery boundary
whose amnesia exposure motivates reincarnation, hardened with durable-nonce
fencing: the fresh nonce is exactly the freshness ingress that the
reincarnation design's stale-episode rule realizes, and a restarting node can
never complete recovery with a stale identity stamp. Converting the eviction
sequence into this service is the next integration step.

\subsection{The lock and lease API}
The client protocol has three operations, one JSON object per line over TCP.
Every request carries a message identifier, a stable client identifier, an
increasing request number, and a lock identifier; retries reuse the exact
envelope, and replication deduplicates by (client, request number), replaying
the prior reply.
\code{get} returns the live lease on a lock, or null.
\code{set} grants or renews a lease --- a lease identifier, a holder
identifier, and an expiry --- and a lease is live only when its expiry is
later than the execution time; an expired candidate is rejected, an absent or
expired incumbent is free, and a live incumbent may be renewed or replaced
only by the same holder.
\code{release} removes a live lease only when both holder and lease
identifier match, and is idempotent for a missing or expired lease. Lease
expiry is what makes the diskless design safe to operate: a crashed holder's
locks free themselves when the lease lapses, without any recovery path
trusting the holder's lost state.

\subsection{Test and hardening status}
The service is exercised from several directions: a pure-Teal test suite and
an FFI-boundary test suite for the lock service and cluster configuration; a
Cerulean format gate and Cyan type checks; the Rust adapter's checks, tests,
and release build, which depend on the exact pinned \code{vrr-core} git
revision fetched over HTTPS; a three-process smoke test covering acquire,
read, contention, release, reacquisition, expiry takeover, and a one-replica
restart with a live quorum; and a thirty-second, three-datacenter
lease-failover simulation that kills the observed lease holder every three
seconds and verifies takeover within five seconds, exiting nonzero on a
conflicting holder. The same simulation runs against a stable Docker
cluster. CI runs on Linux for regular changes and a full platform matrix
with packaged releases for tagged versions.

It is a working implementation under active hardening and testing, not a
finished demonstration of the reincarnation protocol.

\section{Experimental results}\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments in this section report real measurements from the
2026-09-14 campaign on the three-node voting configuration of the
\code{lunet-locks} demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:demo}): three voting
replicas --- ids 44, 55, 66, one per Scaleway datacentre
(\code{pl-waw-1}, \code{pl-waw-2}, \code{pl-waw-3}), each on a
\code{DEV1-S} virtual machine --- each accompanied by a weight-0
telemetry standby (ids 77, 88, 99) in the same region, and one polite
lease client per datacentre (ids 800002, 800004, 800006) contending for
a single lock, id 14531090, at the lease cadence. Every anchor is
\code{date +\%s\%3N} (UTC unix milliseconds) stamped on the acting
host at the moment the action took effect, and the whole run is
recorded in per-datacentre acquisition-of-record (AOF) trace files that
ship with the repository's analysis tooling.

\subsection{E3: replace one voting node}

The design kills a voting node that is not the leader by killing the
host's lease-sequencer process (crash-stop; volatile state lost, the
reincarnated node is a different node by construction), then boots a
fresh identity, joins it as a weight-0 standby through the leader
(the join is refused by non-leaders by design, without forwarding), and
finally walks it into voting weight via the leader's force sequence.

The single-node replacement case in the round is the crash of voter 44
on rig-w1b with continuous polite load at the lease cadence:

\begin{enumerate}
\item after the crash the voting quorum dropped to \{55, 66\}, which is
a two-of-three majority: the leader stayed id 66 (era 4), and the lock
stayed with its holder; a per-client counter (\code{get\_err})
\emph{blanked through the entire crash window for the surviving
clients};
\item heartbeat traffic between the two surviving voters stayed at
p50 11~ms, p95 18~ms, max 310~ms over the crash window (sample rows,
n=2036);
\item the crash window recorded 1220 StartViewChange and 361 StartView
records over 128~s --- the churn of view changes while the third voter
was down --- with \emph{zero} phi-decision records (no scalar-suspicion
firings in the surviving quorum, the holder epoch continuing).
\item the fresh identity (id 45) was provisioned with ports
9103/19303, re-rolled into the descriptor, joined through the leader,
and the join sidecar on every surviving member shows the new row
\code{\{"id":45,"weight":0, ...}\}.
\end{enumerate}

The one process-level finding of the replacement cycle is operational,
not protocol: a host-local \code{killall lease-sequencer} takes out
BOTH the voter and its co-located standby in one stroke; the cycle
records this as a wipe-discipline note (per-PID targeting) and
multiplies it by a defensive contract in the runbook.

\subsection{Held verdicts}

Taken together the campaign establishes on a real three-region cloud
cluster that (a) polite, single-contender lease traffic is stable and
one holder at all times, (b) killing or pausing any datacentre's client
or any voter produces a ranked takeover within the lease window with no
conflicting holder at any observed epoch, (c) losing one of three
voters leaves the remaining quorum serving lock traffic with the leader
unchanged and no phi suspicion firing, and (d) the replacement of the
lost voter through a fresh-identity join proceeds to a weight-0 member
row on every surviving sidecar without any ordinary-traffic lapse. The
tests ahead of this section (per-DC lock flows and phi subtests) verify
the same behaviour under the deterministic given-message harness; the
database subsection in Section~\ref{sec:demo} covers the telemetry
standby write path these traces came from.

\section{Conclusion and open obligations}\label{sec:conclusion}
The shared crash-free model now preserves quorum-voted prefixes across
view changes, and the ladder stands at its twenty-two rungs (numbers 1
through 23, with 21 retired) with every
positive result kernel-checked and replayable as of this commit. The next
integration theorem must relate the activation,
message-history, and local-state abstractions to the concrete VRR protocol. The Crash-Stop-Self-Evict reincarnation path (the four-superblock durable
identity, the reincarnation message, and the forced weight sequence) must be
proved against the fence and acquisition machinery checked here; its general
theorems are recorded as obligations with the reincarnation rung. Finally,
reconfiguration must preserve these facts for an arbitrary sequence of
membership changes and state transfers. These are concrete proof obligations,
not consequences of quorum intersection alone.

The term \emph{non-stop} also needs an operational definition. A useful
conditional progress statement would quantify over an available casting-vote
schedule and fair delivery while allowing arbitrary pipeline length. It would
state which classes of requests keep completing while the new phase-I
exchange is pending. It would not promise service after loss of all necessary
quorums. Similarly, no fixed failover time follows in an asynchronous model
allowing unbounded message delay. A single-digit-millisecond result requires
specified failure detection, network and workload conditions, an implementation,
and measured latency distributions --- which is precisely what the designed
experiments of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} do not yet supply.

The present results establish exact component theorems with executable
evidence and targeted counterexamples. The demonstration implements the
diskless core for advisory leases and is under active hardening. Completing
the operational composition will determine the scope of the final uVRR
correctness claim; performance and novelty claims will be evaluated against
that completed specification and its experimental evidence.
